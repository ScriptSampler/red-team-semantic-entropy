Large language models are increasingly paired with \emph{hallucination detectors}
that estimate whether a generated answer can be trusted. A prominent family is
sampling-based: semantic entropy (SE)~\citep{kuhn2023semantic, farquhar2024detecting}
samples several answers, clusters them by meaning with a natural-language-inference
(NLI) equivalence check, and reports the entropy of the resulting cluster
distribution; semantic reformulation entropy (SRE)~\citep{tong2025sre} extends this
with input reformulations. These detectors are deployed on an implicit promise: that
the uncertainty they report is a property of the \emph{question and answer}, invariant
to how the question happens to be phrased.

That invariance has been asserted but not adversarially tested \emph{on the detector
itself}. Prior red-teaming of LLMs targets the model's answer---eliciting
hallucinations~\citep{liang2025seca, liang2026realista}---or evades text-origin
classifiers by paraphrasing~\citep{cheng2025advparaphrasing, fang2025copa}; work on
uncertainty manipulation has used fine-tuning or backdoors rather than a
meaning-preserving input change~\citep{uncertaintyfragile2024}, and the closest
detector-side attack degrades a hidden-state probe model-side~\citep{corvus2026}. The
question we ask is different: can an attacker who only \emph{rephrases the question},
under a hard semantic-equivalence constraint, move a sampling-based detector's score
in a chosen direction while the model's answer---and its correctness---stays put?

We show the invariance promise is breakable, and we are deliberate about what
``breakable'' requires evidence for. We attack in \emph{both} directions: the
\emph{hide} attack suppresses the score of a wrong answer so a hallucination is
missed, and the under-explored \emph{false-alarm} attack inflates the score of a
\emph{correct} answer so it is flagged---arguably the more damaging failure for a
deployed abstention system, and cheaper for an adversary who merely wants to make a
model look unreliable. Crucially, we hold three things fixed that prior evaluations do
not jointly control: (i) targets are selected \emph{score-independently} so the clean
detector is not evaluated on a pool its own scores defined (a circularity that inflates
clean AUROC to $1.0$); (ii) an attack counts only if the model's \emph{hallucination
status} is unchanged under the paraphrase, checked with the same oracle that labelled
the data; and (iii) effect sizes are reported \emph{net of a noise floor}, since the
detector score is a finite-sample estimate and an optimiser that selects the extremum
over many candidates can otherwise mistake sampling variance for a successful attack.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1) A bidirectional, NLI-constrained paraphrase attack on sampling-based hallucination
detectors, foregrounding the induced-false-positive direction. (2) A circularity-free,
null-controlled evaluation protocol---score-independent target selection, an
answer-invariance success criterion, and a benign-paraphrase noise floor---whose
three controls we show are each load-bearing: without them the clean detector looks
perfect, sampling noise masquerades as attack success, and meaning-changing
paraphrases are miscounted as wins. (3) A measurement of
transferability across the detector family (SE$\leftrightarrow$SRE, and toward a
hidden-state probe), with an explicit account of the shared-NLI confound that bounds
what transfer can establish. (4) A reformulation-averaging defense and an
analysis of what it does and does not close. Our headline effect size on the fair,
null-controlled pool is reported in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. [Quantitative result
