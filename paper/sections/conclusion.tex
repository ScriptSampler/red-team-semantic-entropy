Sampling-based hallucination detectors treat semantic uncertainty as a property of the
question, invariant to surface form. We set out to test that assumption adversarially and
found that the more informative obstacle lay upstream of the adversary: at the sample
budgets these detectors are actually run at, the score has very little room in which to be
attacked. A tenth of correct answers are already at the estimator's maximum, a quarter are
in its top decile, and the whole separation between correct and hallucinating answers is a
fraction of the estimator's own dispersion. Pushing a correct answer across a flagging
threshold, in the under-explored false-alarm direction we foreground, is therefore cheap---
and frequently achieved by ordinary rephrasing, not only by an optimiser.

Establishing even that much honestly turned out to require more care than the question
first appears to need. Because the attack reports a maximum over a large candidate search,
about half of its apparent effect is selection on estimator noise, which we measure directly
by re-scoring the selected paraphrase on independent samples. Because the score saturates,
effect sizes in its native units are censored on roughly half of attacked targets, and a
null must remain valid when attack and benign candidates tie at the cap. And because the
detector's clusterer is the same model that certifies our paraphrases as
meaning-preserving, no single-clusterer measurement can separate a real change in the
model's answers from that model's own inconsistency. We report the confirmatory evaluation
under a protocol built to survive all three, and confine claims to what it supports: targets
chosen without reference to the detector's own scores, success requiring the hallucination
status to survive the paraphrase, and a null that prices the attacker's search budget in
rather than comparing a maximum against single draws.

Two boundaries remain, and we state them as the work to do rather than as caveats to
wave away. First, our clusterer and our equivalence gate are the same NLI model, so
whether the vulnerability is a property of the \emph{paradigm} or of one model's
self-inconsistency turns on an independent clustering oracle. We build one: the cheap
candidates (exact match, sentence embeddings) fail on the adversarial case, but a
victim- and NLI-independent LLM-judge, validated on a domain-matched proxy of that case
($0.93$ vs.\ $0.51$), clears it---and it is under this judge that the confirmatory large-$n$ run,
now under way, adjudicates whether the false-alarm effect survives the independent clusterer (the
direction to which the judge is re-scoped; Methods), a
verdict we draw from that run rather than from the machinery-validation sample. Second,
the meaning-preserving guarantee rests on an automatic gate; a human equivalence audit
is needed before it is fully established. Whether a simple reformulation-averaging
defense confers measurable robustness or merely raises the attacker's query cost is an
open question we frame but do not yet settle; either way, a detector whose own
reformulation step is its most natural defense suggests that robustness to paraphrase
must be designed into these detectors rather than assumed. Taken together, the results argue that ``semantic'' uncertainty
inherits the blind spots of whatever model defines its notion of meaning, and that
detectors built on a learned equivalence relation should be evaluated as the
adversarial targets they now are.
